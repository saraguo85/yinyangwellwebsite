The "healthy" summer plate TCM would quietly side-eye

By Sara · 6 min read · What to go easy on: cold, raw and hard to digest

[ iced latte, raw salad, yoghurt, cold melon ]

Picture a summer lunch that looks like a wellness ad: an iced oat-milk latte, a big raw salad, a pot of yoghurt, and cold melon straight from the fridge. Wholesome, right?

From the view of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), that exact plate is doing your body a quiet disservice. Not because any one thing is "bad", but because of how much cold and raw it asks your digestion to handle at once.

I'm not a practitioner. I've just spent years living this tradition and watching how it plays out. Here's the one idea that reframes half of what we're told is healthy, and the three groups of foods it asks you to go easy on.

The one principle: your digestion runs on warmth

In Traditional Chinese Medicine, the system that turns food into usable energy is pictured as a small warm fire in your middle. Everything you eat has to be "cooked down" by that fire before your body can use it. Anything that arrives cold, raw, or heavy makes the fire work harder. And if it's already running low, food sits, ferments and turns into what TCM calls "damp": that heavy, bloated, foggy, sluggish feeling.

So this isn't about calories or clean eating. It's about not making your digestion work against you. Three groups matter most.

1. Cold and iced

The clearest one. Iced drinks, cold brew, smoothies straight from the blender, ice cream, cold water with every meal, fruit and melon eaten fridge-cold. Each one drops a bucket of cold on that warm fire right when it's trying to work.

The very British summer traps to notice: cold beer with a barbecue, and cold watermelon in an air-conditioned room. Both stack cold on cold.

The easy swap: room-temperature or warm drinks, and letting cold food come up from fridge-cold before you eat it. Yes, warm drinks in summer. It sounds wrong, but they cool you more steadily than ice, which just makes your body work harder to warm back up.

2. Raw, including a word on dairy

Raw food is cold food your body then has to warm and break down from scratch. Big raw salads, raw cucumber, undercooked eggs, rare prawns. Traditional Chinese Medicine leans strongly toward cooked and warm: gently steamed, stir-fried, simmered, especially if your digestion feels temperamental.

Dairy sits here too, and it's the one that surprises people. In TCM, milk, yoghurt, cheese and milky drinks are seen as cooling and damp-forming, easy to turn into that heavy, congested feeling in people who are prone to it. Plenty of people digest dairy perfectly well and needn't give it a second thought. But if you often find yourself with a blocked nose, phlegm you're always clearing, bloating, loose stools, a heavy or weary body, sluggish stools or brain fog, dairy is one of the first things worth easing off for a week or two, just to see if you feel lighter. It's not a rule about dairy. It's an experiment on yourself.

Fruit gets a gentler version of the same note. TCM doesn't say skip it. It says go easy, and time it well: a modest amount, ideally earlier in the day, and local and in season where you can, rather than a cold fruit-and-smoothie habit on an already-cold gut.

3. Hard to digest

Some foods are simply a lot of work to break down, so TCM treats them as "heavy": fine in small amounts when you're well, worth easing off when you're run down. The usual suspects: beans and soy (including tofu, the softer, gypsum-set kind especially), wholegrains and mixed grains, sweetcorn, potato, pumpkin, sweet potato and taro, sticky rice, peanuts and dried nuts, lotus root and wood-ear, glass noodles, and processed things like fish balls, meatballs and mock-meats. Even eggs land differently: gently fried or scrambled is easier going than hard-boiled.

None of these are forbidden. They're just not what you want to pile on when your fire is low.

The real trouble isn't any single food. It's stacking: cold and raw and heavy, all in one meal.

The nuance that makes this liveable: it's load, not a lifetime ban

Here's the part people miss. Traditional Chinese Medicine isn't asking you to never touch these again. Almost anything is fine in the right amount, at the right time. The real trouble is stacking: a single day or meal that piles cold and raw and hard-to-digest together. Sweetcorn at midday? Fine. Cucumber, stir-fried? Fine. An egg, cooked well? Fine. But cold cucumber, sweetcorn and a hard-boiled egg, over an iced drink, is the combination that turns into "damp".

And you can genuinely tip the odds back in your favour:

  • Cook it. Heat does some of the breaking-down before it reaches you.
  • Add warming spices. Ginger, spring onion, pepper, a little cinnamon give the fire a hand.
  • Time it well. Save the heavier or cooler foods for midday, when your own warmth is at its peak, not last thing at night.
  • Movement, sunshine and a calm, unhurried meal all help your body process food, too.

So the takeaway isn't a list of bans. It's a lens: when you're well, enjoy widely; when you're run down, keep it warm, cooked and simple, and don't stack the cold on top. And the honest caveat that every one of our doctors would add: which of these actually matters depends on your own body, so it's worth choosing food to suit yours rather than following any list wholesale.
